## TL;DR

> Build a product you genuinely need, then protect its durability by keeping the team, costs, and planning horizon small. The speaker argues that this makes the business easier to sustain and keeps product decisions close to real users.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start with a recurring problem you personally experience and build the smallest solution you would willingly rely on.
2. Treat cost as the main controllable competitor: the speaker’s basic survival rule is to earn more than you spend.
3. Keep the company thin and the product substantial; require every new layer of overhead or organization to justify itself.
4. Use teams of roughly two people per feature—often one programmer and one designer—to reduce translation loss and keep scope comprehensible.
5. Plan in short horizons of about six weeks, choose a direction, and correct course through small daily decisions instead of detailed multi-year forecasts.
6. Seek a stable, profitable orbit rather than growth for its own sake; evaluate opportunities by whether they make the product or company thicker, slower, or less enjoyable to run.
7. Maintain direct customer contact through support, onboarding messages, and email so decision-makers remain exposed to actual user experiences.

## Overview

This lesson captures a product philosophy centered on building tools you genuinely want to use, keeping the business thin, and treating cost control as the main constraint. In the source, the speaker argues that durable software companies come from small teams, direct customer contact, restrained growth, and repeated simplification over time. The lesson is practical rather than universal: it reflects one founder's operating style at 37signals, not a claim that every company should be run this way.

## Key Concepts

- **Build for yourself first**: The speaker's earliest software came from a personal need: tracking a music collection and loaned CDs. The core claim is that if you make something you truly want, there are likely enough similar people to form a viable market.
- **Your real competition is cost**: The source frames business simply: survive by making more than you spend. Because competitors' actions are outside your control, the speaker treats operating cost, overhead, and organizational bloat as the main variables worth managing closely.
- **Thin company, thick product**: A recurring metaphor separates the 'envelope' from the 'letter': the business structure exists to carry the product, not overshadow it. The desired shape is a minimal organizational shell supporting substantial, well-made products.
- **Small teams reduce miscommunication**: The source describes teams of roughly two people per feature, often one programmer and one designer. The claimed benefit is less translation loss, fewer layers, tighter scope, and products with smaller, more comprehensible surface area.
- **Aim for orbit, not infinite growth**: Instead of chasing a perpetual hockey-stick curve, the speaker prefers reaching a stable, profitable state and maintaining it. Growth is useful to escape early constraints, but not valuable for its own sake.
- **Plan by direction, not detailed long-range prediction**: The source rejects detailed multi-year planning in favor of short horizons, often around six weeks, with day-by-day course correction. The metaphor is a squirrel: it knows roughly where it is headed, then adjusts continuously.
- **Stay close to users**: The speaker emphasizes direct emails, founder-written onboarding letters, and regular customer support contact. The lesson is that product judgment improves when the people making decisions are exposed to the people using the product.
- **Authenticity can be a product choice**: The source favors leaving minor imperfections in demos and writing in a direct, personal voice. One supporting anecdote about Navajo rugs is explicitly secondhand in the interview, so it should be treated as illustrative rather than verified history.

## How It Works

Use this philosophy as an operating loop. Start with a problem you personally feel. Build the smallest tool that solves it well enough for you to rely on it. Keep the team small enough that the people shaping the product can still see the whole system. Limit planning to a short horizon with a clear direction, then make many small decisions instead of a few giant ones. Review the business through two lenses: first, does the product become simpler or better for actual users; second, does the company remain cheap enough and clear enough to keep making the product for a long time. Prefer direct contact with customers over layered reporting, and treat organizational additions as guilty until proven necessary. If growth opportunities appear, evaluate them against the risk of making the product, team, or company thicker, slower, or less enjoyable to run.

## Training Exercise

Pick one tool you use weekly that annoys you. Write a one-page note with five sections: 1. the exact frustration you personally experience, 2. the smallest version of a product that would solve it for you, 3. the two-person team you would assign to build it, 4. the costs and organizational layers you would refuse to add in the first year, and 5. a six-week plan made of weekly checkpoints rather than a long roadmap. Then answer three review questions: 'Would I use this myself?', 'What part of this got thicker than necessary?', and 'If this stayed small but profitable, would that be enough?'

Competitors’ actions cannot be controlled, but spending, overhead, and organizational complexity can. The speaker argues that keeping costs below revenue is the most direct way to preserve the company’s ability to survive.

<details><summary>What does “thin company, thick product” mean?</summary>

The organization should be a minimal shell that supports a substantial, well-made product. Processes, management layers, and overhead should not become more prominent or complicated than the product they exist to deliver.

</details>

Use small teams—often a programmer and a designer—and work in short horizons of roughly six weeks. Set a general direction, then adjust through frequent small decisions informed by direct contact with users.
