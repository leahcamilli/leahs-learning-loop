## TL;DR

> A frontier team separates rapid AI experimentation from dependable day-to-day execution, then converts the few useful experiments into maintainable tools and workflows. Its value comes from integration—not from running the largest number of experiments.

## Key Takeaways

1. Create a small frontier team so most employees can focus on their work without personally tracking every model and tool release.
2. Pair “pirates,” who explore quickly and tolerate messy prototypes, with “architects,” who make promising prototypes reliable and maintainable.
3. Evaluate models on samples of your organization’s real work; Every calls these reusable personal benchmarks.
4. Assign a wrangler to organize experiments, synthesize lessons, monitor costs, and translate useful discoveries into products, content, or services.
5. Protect colleagues’ attention by packaging experimental results into concise, auditable explanations instead of flooding shared channels with raw updates.
6. Build personalized tools around real user needs—such as a distraction-limiting browser or an audience-aware writing assistant—rather than experimenting for novelty alone.
7. Judge the team by whether useful practices are understood and adopted across the organization, not by how many prototypes it produces.

## Overview

A frontier team is a small group tasked with testing emerging AI capabilities and translating the useful results for the rest of an organization. Every presents this as a separation of concerns: the frontier team explores what has become possible while product and operating teams continue delivering dependable work.

This model is relevant to leaders, engineers, operators, writers, and AI-adoption teams. The examples show that the hard part is not merely producing prototypes; it is evaluating them on real work, controlling costs and information overload, hardening promising systems, and earning adoption.

## Key Concepts

- **Separation of concerns**: The frontier team absorbs the burden of following new models and running uncertain experiments. Other teams can keep executing while receiving only the findings mature enough to deserve their attention.
- **Pirates and architects**: “Pirates” move quickly, test many ideas, and accept disposable or messy prototypes. “Architects” structure the promising work, choose appropriate technical foundations, and improve reliability, deployment, and maintainability.
- **Personal benchmarks**: A personal benchmark is a small, saved set of someone’s real work that can be rerun with different models. The presenter describes using these benchmarks to move beyond qualitative “vibe checks” and attach more consistent evidence to model evaluations.
- **Frontier wrangling**: A wrangler creates enough process to capture experiments without suppressing creativity. At Every, the role includes synthesizing themes, directing exploration, tracking token usage and estimated cost, and translating lessons for other parts of the business.
- **Attention protection**: Faster AI-assisted production can generate more material than colleagues can absorb. Experimenters therefore need to compress their work into digestible explanations and avoid turning shared channels into streams of unfiltered ideas.
- **Context-rich AI workflows**: Mike Taylor reports better results after allowing AI systems to search relevant Slack messages, meeting notes, and other work context. His explainer-video example preserved links back to source material, illustrating why access should be paired with provenance and auditability.
- **Personalized applications**: Several team members built tools around their own workflows: a tabless browser, an audience-aware writing plugin, word processors, and an agent orchestrator. The lesson is to begin with a concrete friction point and test whether a tailored application changes the work meaningfully.
- **Integration as the success metric**: Experiments have limited organizational value if no one understands or adopts them. The team should listen to users, select a small number of promising patterns, package them clearly, and integrate them into actual operations.

## How It Works

1. Establish a mandate: define which emerging capabilities the team may explore and which dependable workflows remain owned by operating teams.

2. Form complementary roles: include rapid experimenters, technical architects, a wrangler, and people who understand the target workflows deeply.

3. Collect real test cases: save representative examples of actual work, expected qualities, constraints, and failure conditions as reusable benchmarks.

4. Run bounded experiments: state the question, model or tool, time and token budget, expected output, and stopping condition before building.

5. Preserve evidence: retain prompts, outputs, source references, costs, decisions, and user observations so results can be audited and compared.

6. Triage outcomes: discard weak ideas, publish lessons from informative failures, and send promising prototypes to architectural review.

7. Harden selected work: improve reliability, maintainability, deployment, privacy controls, documentation, and fit with existing workflows.

8. Integrate and measure: introduce the result to a small user group, gather feedback, monitor continued usage and value, and expand only when it sticks.

## Training Exercise

1. Choose one recurring workflow that currently consumes attention, such as reviewing drafts, summarizing meetings, or preparing customer updates.

2. Assemble five representative examples of the real work. For each, record what a good result must contain and which failures would make it unusable.

3. Give one person a two-hour “pirate” session to test two AI-assisted approaches. Require a record of prompts, outputs, elapsed time, source access, and estimated cost.

4. Have a second person act as the architect. Ask them to identify reliability, security, maintenance, integration, and user-experience problems in the stronger prototype.

5. Have a wrangler produce a one-page synthesis: the original problem, evidence, cost, limitations, recommended decision, and the smallest next test.

6. Pilot the revised workflow with two intended users for one week. Measure whether they use it voluntarily, how much time it saves, and what corrections remain necessary.

7. Decide explicitly to discard it, continue experimenting, or harden it for adoption. Share only the distilled finding and supporting evidence with the broader organization.

## Test Yourself

<details><summary>Why should a frontier team be separate from the rest of an organization without becoming isolated from it?</summary>

Separation lets specialists explore fast-changing AI capabilities without distracting everyone else. The team must still return useful findings to the organization as understandable, maintainable tools, workflows, content, or services.

</details>

<details><summary>How do pirates, architects, and a wrangler address different failure modes of experimentation?</summary>

Pirates prevent excessive caution by exploring many possibilities quickly. Architects prevent fragile prototypes from becoming operational dependencies, while the wrangler prevents lessons, costs, and promising experiments from getting lost or overwhelming colleagues.

</details>

<details><summary>What makes an AI experiment suitable for wider adoption?</summary>

It should solve a real workflow problem, perform well on representative work, and be understandable and maintainable. It also needs an owner, a cost justification, and a measured path for gathering feedback from the people expected to use it.

</details>
