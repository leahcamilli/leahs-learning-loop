## TL;DR

> As AI makes features, integrations, and custom software easier to reproduce, durable SaaS advantage shifts from code to trust, operational dependence, authority, collective learning, and difficult-to-run infrastructure. Audit how AI could weaken both your product’s supply and its customers’ demand, then strengthen the moats a competitor cannot simply generate.

## Key Takeaways

1. Assume customers need only the few features they use—not a complete replica—to replace part of your product.
2. Evaluate AI risk on both sides of the market: six supply-side attacks increase alternatives, while four demand-side attacks reduce customers, seats, workflows, or human users.
3. Features, proprietary code, integrations, UX familiarity, and deliberately difficult data export are becoming weaker defenses as AI reduces implementation and migration effort.
4. Eight stronger moats are buyer trust, system-of-record status, authority to act, unchecked trusted output, official approval, multi-party coordination, cross-customer learning, and operations that are difficult to maintain.
5. A learning loop is defensible only when each customer improves outcomes for others, customers cannot export the accumulated learning, and the system continues learning.
6. Move beyond advice when appropriate: software authorized to make payments, intervene in cancellations, or perform another consequential action can be harder to replace than a reporting tool.
7. Treat the operational work you resent—spam control, deliverability, proxy rotation, compliance, or infrastructure reliability—as a possible moat rather than merely overhead.

## Overview

AI expands the supply of software by helping customers, competitors, open-source projects, platforms, and agencies build alternatives faster. It can also reduce demand when customers disappear, employee seats contract, managed workflows become obsolete, or AI agents replace the humans who previously operated the software. The presenter estimates that 10–20% of SaaS companies could decline or disappear, explicitly framing that number as his informed guess rather than a measured forecast.

The practical response is to build advantages outside reproducible code. This framework is aimed at SaaS founders, product leaders, operators, and buyers evaluating whether a product will remain valuable when feature development and migration become cheaper.

## Key Concepts

- **Buyer trust and distribution relationships**: A strong brand becomes more defensible when accountants, agencies, partners, or internal champions have built careers or businesses around recommending it. QuickBooks and Shopify illustrate how an ecosystem can keep a technically comparable newcomer outside the buying conversation.
- **System of record**: A system of record holds an organization’s authoritative operational state: history, approvals, balances, statuses, roles, rules, and integrations. Jira and Gymdesk illustrate products whose value comes from being woven into daily work, making replacement an organizational migration rather than a simple data export.
- **Authority to act**: An application that executes a payment, reimbursement, or retention offer has more operational authority than one that only produces a recommendation. Stripe Connect, Reimbi, and Churnkey are presented as examples of software permitted to act directly within consequential workflows.
- **Trusted output that goes unchecked**: Some products earn enough trust that customers stop independently verifying their output. The presenter uses Gusto and Avalara as examples: long-term reliability in payroll or tax work makes an unproven AI-generated replacement unattractive even if its visible features look similar.
- **Official approval**: Licenses, certifications, carrier registration, accreditation, and regulatory approvals impose requirements that copying code does not satisfy. Serving regulated customers alone is insufficient; the stronger moat exists when the vendor itself has earned an approval a newcomer must obtain.
- **Multi-party coordination**: Replacement becomes harder when an entire company, community, league, or customer network must move together. Slack and Commit Swimming illustrate how shared communication and coordination increase switching difficulty beyond the preferences of a single user.
- **Cross-customer learning loop**: A learning loop compounds when one customer’s outcomes improve predictions or decisions for every other customer. Stripe Radar and Mailchimp’s Omnivore are cited as examples in fraud and spam detection, where a new entrant’s isolated dataset is less useful than the incumbent’s accumulated learning.
- **Operational burden**: Some services are easy to prototype but persistently difficult to operate. ScrapingBee, Cloudflare, and Drip illustrate burdens such as rotating proxies, avoiding blocks, maintaining infrastructure, controlling spam, and protecting deliverability; the ongoing burden discourages both self-hosting and shallow competitors.

## How It Works

1. Map the ten AI attack vectors.
   - Supply: customers build the features they need; competitors reach parity faster; open-source projects improve; host platforms bundle the feature; agencies offer custom software at SaaS-like prices; or a foundation model absorbs the function.
   - Demand: customers go out of business; employee seats contract; the managed activity disappears; or AI agents replace the humans who used the product.

2. Discount weakening classic moats. Do not assume features, clever code, integrations, familiar UX, owned traffic, or export friction will remain as protective as they were before AI-assisted development and migration.

3. Score the eight stronger moats. Ask who recommends you, whether you hold authoritative workflow state, whether you act or advise, whether customers verify your output, what approvals you hold, who must coordinate a switch, whether customers improve outcomes for one another, and what ongoing burden you uniquely carry.

4. Choose an adjacent move rather than attempting all eight. A reporting tool might execute an approved action; a point solution might capture the definitive workflow; a data product might create a shared learning loop; or a vertical tool might cultivate trusted industry partners.

5. Validate the moat behaviorally. Look for customers delegating decisions, integrating downstream systems, coordinating multiple participants, trusting results without rechecking, or declining to reproduce the operational burden themselves.

## Training Exercise

1. List your SaaS product’s three most-used customer workflows and identify the smallest useful replacement a customer or competitor could build.
2. Create two columns labeled “supply” and “demand.” Score each of the ten attack vectors from 0 (irrelevant) to 5 (existential), adding one concrete piece of evidence or uncertainty for every score.
3. Score each of the eight moats from 0 to 5. For every score above zero, name observable proof—for example, an approval held, a downstream dependency, multiple parties who must migrate, or a decision customers no longer verify.
4. Select the highest-risk attack and one realistically adjacent moat. Write a 90-day experiment that connects them, such as adding an authorized action to a reporting workflow or centralizing approvals and status history to become the system of record.
5. Define one measurable signal of increased defensibility: partner-sourced sales, workflows completed automatically, integrations depending on authoritative data, retained accounts after a competitor comparison, or improved predictions from pooled outcomes.
6. Re-score the matrix after 90 days. Keep the initiative only if customer behavior—not merely additional features—shows that replacement has become harder.

## Test Yourself

<details><summary>Why is matching an incumbent’s complete feature set the wrong way to assess AI-enabled competition?</summary>

A customer or competitor may need to reproduce only the few features used in a particular workflow. AI can shorten that narrower path to adequate parity even when rebuilding the entire application remains difficult.

</details>

<details><summary>What separates an ordinary collection of customer data from a defensible learning loop?</summary>

Each customer’s experience must improve the product for other customers, the resulting learning must remain with the product even if raw data is exported, and the system must keep improving. Siloed or stagnant data does not satisfy those conditions.

</details>

<details><summary>How can a SaaS company turn a point solution into a stronger system-of-record moat?</summary>

It can absorb more of the customer’s daily workflow, including statuses, approvals, balances, history, roles, rules, and integrations. Replacement then requires rebuilding operating processes and downstream connections, not merely importing records.

</details>
