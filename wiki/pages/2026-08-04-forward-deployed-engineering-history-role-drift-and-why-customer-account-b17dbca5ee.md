## TL;DR

> Forward deployed engineering is best understood as accountability for customer outcomes, not a fixed job description. That distinction matters because the role has accumulated responsibilities—from platform stability and data integration to custom solutions, enablement, and AI outcome ownership—without shedding the old ones.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define deployed engineering by its customer feedback loop and outcome ownership, not by titles such as FDE, agent engineer, solutions engineer, or customer engineer.
2. Map the role across five buckets: platform stability, data integration, custom solutioning, customer enablement, and outcome ownership.
3. Treat data integration as core deployment work; the speaker argues that a data platform provides little value until customer data is connected and modeled usefully.
4. Move recurring customer-specific solutions into the core product so the deployed role does not become an unbounded collection of custom work.
5. Use enablement to multiply customer capability rather than keeping every implementation task permanently dependent on the deployed engineer.
6. Exploit cheaper AI-assisted coding to let customer-facing engineers prototype solutions and influence product development more directly, according to the speaker.
7. Assign explicit responsibility for measurable results when pricing is outcome-based, because delivering software or seats alone does not ensure the promised outcome.

## Overview

This lesson explains forward deployed engineering (FDE) through the speaker's historical account, mainly anchored in Palantir from 2008 to 2026 and extended to Sierra's view of agent engineering. The core claim is intentionally provocative: FDE no longer names a single clear job. Instead, it has accumulated several responsibilities over time: platform stability, data integration, custom solution building, customer enablement, and now end-to-end outcome ownership in AI systems. A practical takeaway is that the durable skill is not a job title but the ability to connect customer problems, product gaps, deployment realities, and measurable outcomes.

## Key Concepts

- **FDE began as on-the-ground deployment work**: In the transcript, 'forward deployed' originally meant sitting with customers and operating close to their environment, partly because deployments were on-prem. Early work looked more like DevOps and incident response than the stereotype of a strategic field engineer.
- **Role expansion happened in layers**: The speaker describes a timeline: early platform stability work, then data integration, then custom solutions such as dashboards, then customer enablement. The important lesson is that new duties were added without old ones disappearing.
- **Customer accountability is the stable throughline**: Although the tasks changed, the speaker argues that FDEs consistently owned customer outcomes. That accountability linked technical work such as infrastructure, data modeling, solution design, and training.
- **Data integration made the platform useful**: The talk argues that a data platform without integrated customer data is not very valuable. FDEs were therefore responsible not just for running software, but for connecting messy real-world systems and modeling the data in a usable structure.
- **Custom solutions bridged product gaps**: Once data was available, FDEs built practical interfaces and workflows, for example with Palantir's Slate. This shows a common pattern: deployed engineers often turn a general platform into a customer-specific solution before the product fully matures.
- **Enablement turned services into platform leverage**: By around 2020, the speaker says the work expanded to helping customers do more themselves. This reframed FDE from pure implementation toward teaching, platform adoption, and multiplying customer capability.
- **Cheap code increases the value of customer-facing engineering**: The talk claims that coding agents make it easier to prototype and ship solutions, which lets engineers closer to customers influence product more directly. This is partly interpretive, but it is a central thesis of the talk.
- **Outcome-based pricing strengthens the FDE model**: The speaker connects AI agents and outcome-based pricing: if customers pay for results rather than seats, someone must ensure those results are achieved. In the talk, that 'someone' is effectively the forward deployed engineer or adjacent role.

## How It Works

Use this framework to understand or design a deployed engineering role. First, map the work into five buckets named in the talk: platform stability, data integration, custom solutioning, customer enablement, and outcome ownership. Second, ask which of those buckets your team actually expects one person to cover. Third, separate title from function: the transcript argues that 'FDE' is often a bundle of jobs rather than a precise discipline. Fourth, evaluate the role by its customer feedback loop. If the engineer is expected to hear customer pain directly, translate it into product or operational changes, and be accountable for results, the role is functioning in the FDE tradition even if it has a different title such as agent engineer, solutions engineer, or customer engineer. One caution from the source: many company examples and historical characterizations are presented by the speaker from personal experience, not backed by external evidence in the transcript itself.

## Training Exercise

Pick one real or hypothetical AI product and write a one-page FDE role definition for it. Include: 1. one task in platform stability, 2. one task in data integration, 3. one task in custom solutioning, 4. one task in customer enablement, and 5. one measurable customer outcome the engineer would own. Then answer two questions: Which of these tasks should stay human-led even if coding becomes cheap? Which tasks should move into the core product so the role does not become an unbounded catch-all?
